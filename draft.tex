\abstract{Uncertain ground conditions and buried utilities complicate shield tunnelling, while boreholes and surface investigations may provide incomplete coverage in dense urban settings. Cosmic-ray muography offers a passive means of investigating subsurface density, but field deployment requires instrumentation that can operate under construction vibration, elevated temperature, high humidity and restricted access. This paper presents a field-deployable three-layer Micromegas muography system and two applications on Shenzhen Metro Line~22. The system incorporates environmental control, pneumatic vibration isolation, a low-consumption gas supply, condition monitoring and integrated data acquisition for both mobile and stationary measurements. Aboard tunnel boring machines, it operated at depths of about 15--30~m over 1371 rings; on the right line, acquisition availability was 85.2\%, or 97.2\% excluding external power and network interruptions, and platform vibration was not detected inside the isolated enclosure. Measurements at two shield positions were combined with tunnel geometry and borehole logs to extend the borehole density model into three dimensions, indicating a possible low-density region above and to one side of the tunnel. In a separate investigation, a disused pipeline that ground-penetrating radar had failed to locate was surveyed from four positions in the completed parallel tunnel; position-specific U-Net models and multi-view geometry estimated its axis. On this basis the construction team adjusted the tunnel alignment and passed the location without interference, although the pipe position was not verified directly. The deployments demonstrate the field feasibility and decision-support value of muography during tunnel construction, while highlighting the need for faster imaging, matched reference measurements and uncertainty quantification.}

\chapter{Subsurface imaging in shield tunnelling and pipeline localisation via a robust cosmic-ray muography system: a field study}

\section{INTRODUCTION}
Unexpected ground conditions and buried utilities are among the main risks in urban shield tunnelling: a weak zone, cavity or obstruction ahead of the face can interrupt advance and damage surrounding infrastructure. Boreholes give only point information, and surface investigations are often restricted in dense urban areas. Cosmic-ray muography addresses this gap from inside the tunnel. Atmospheric muons penetrate tens to hundreds of metres of ground, and an underground detector records how many arrive from each direction; comparing this with an open-sky reference gives the density integrated along each path \autocite{procureur2018}. Combining views from several directions and positions with tunnel geometry and borehole information turns these measurements into a three-dimensional picture of density variations around and ahead of the excavation. The method is passive, requires no radiation source, surface access or contact with the target, and can operate while construction continues.

Previous underground studies have reconstructed overburden density and rock--air interfaces from multiple detector locations \autocite{guardincerri2017}, identified concealed construction shafts and a subsequently confirmed overburden void in a railway tunnel \autocite{thompson2020}, and detected structural changes along a subway station and tunnel \autocite{mao2023}. These studies establish the potential of tunnel-based measurements, but sustained deployment during active shield construction introduces additional instrumentation and operational requirements.

Mounting a detector on an operating tunnel boring machine (TBM) exposes it to vibration, heat and humidity in a confined space with limited maintenance access, and the observation geometry changes as the shield advances. A stationary investigation instead requires the instrument to be repositioned accurately to combine several views of a fixed target.

This paper presents a field-deployable three-layer Micromegas muography platform supporting both dynamic TBM-mounted measurements and stationary multi-position tunnel investigations. The contributions are: (1) a system designed for construction conditions; (2) an assessment of sustained operation on Shenzhen Metro Line~22; and (3) two field demonstrations, comprising TBM-mounted ground-density imaging and a four-position investigation of a suspected buried pipeline. 

\section{MUOGRAPHY SYSTEM AND FIELD PERFORMANCE}
\subsection{Detector and tracking principle}
The tracking unit comprises three thermally bonded Micromegas layers, each with a $40\times40$~cm$^2$ sensitive area and separated by approximately 15~cm \autocite{feng2021thermal}. Orthogonal readout strips segmented at 1.6~mm give two-dimensional hit positions on each layer, and each muon trajectory is reconstructed from the hits in all three layers. This fine segmentation provides an angular resolution of about $0.3^\circ$, among the best available for a detector with such a large sensitive area, so fine directional detail is resolved without sacrificing acceptance. The front-end and data acquisition electronics were developed in-house and customised for muography, forming inter-layer coincidences and aggregating events in a compact readout chain \autocite{wang2025readout,liu2025backend}. Table~\ref{tab:specs} summarises the main specifications of the deployed system.

\subsection{Field-compatible system design}
Shield tunnelling exposes the instrument to heat and humidity, machine vibration, a continuously changing observation geometry and limited access for maintenance. The IP56 main enclosure integrates the detectors, readout electronics, power supplies and a sealed air-conditioning unit that controls internal temperature and humidity. For TBM-mounted operation, four pneumatic isolators separate the inclined detector frame from the shield support platform, and vibration sensors on the platform and inside the enclosure record the vibration on each side of the isolators. An inclinometer records the detector inclination so that each measurement can be associated with its observation geometry. A dedicated gas-control subsystem in a separate enclosure supplies the argon--carbon-dioxide mixture with much lower consumption than laboratory flow-gas operation; a single 8~L cylinder at 10~MPa sustained approximately three months of field operation, which set the routine maintenance interval. The system is a headless instrument: it runs without an operator on site, and its status, environmental and vibration data are monitored and controlled remotely over Ethernet or RS485, making it suitable for autonomous, long-term sensing in the tunnel. Figure~\ref{fig:installation} shows the enclosure design with dimensions (a), internal arrangement (b) and installation on the TBM (c).

\subsection{Field deployment and operational performance}
The system operated at depths of approximately 15--30~m in the Guihua Station--Songyuanxia Station interval from 6~November~2025 to 27~July~2026, covering 1371 rings (428 on the left line and 943 on the right). It was installed approximately 5~m behind the cutterhead on the left line and 15~m behind it on the right. Detector positions and orientations were associated with the corresponding ring records for subsequent analysis. Monitored enclosure temperatures remained below $40\,^{\circ}$C and relative humidity below 70\%.

On the right line, normal acquisition totalled 2965.5~h, or 85.2\% of the 3480.0~h monitoring record. The availability was 97.2\% after 401.5~h of external power loss and 28.3~h of network interruption were excluded. The remaining 84.7~h comprised system maintenance.

Figure~\ref{fig:vibration} shows a 34~h record from 14--15 February 2026. Platform vibration began at 19:15 and continued for the remaining 14.7~h, with a typical peak particle velocity (PPV, vector sum of three axes) of 0.21~mm/s, 99\% of readings below 2.8~mm/s and a maximum of 3.7~mm/s. No vibration was recorded inside the enclosure. The reconstructed three-layer track rate was 13.54~min$^{-1}$ in the 19.2~h before platform vibration began and 13.34~min$^{-1}$ during it, a difference of about 1.2 standard errors, and its 5-min fluctuations (1.46 and 1.60~min$^{-1}$) were consistent with counting statistics alone (about 1.6~min$^{-1}$). Within this record, platform vibration was therefore not transmitted to the detectors at a detectable level, and detector operation was not measurably affected.

\section{FIELD DEMONSTRATION I: TBM-MOUNTED GROUND-DENSITY IMAGING}
\subsection{Engineering objective}
Borehole logs describe the ground only at discrete points, and the strata between them are normally interpolated. The objective of this demonstration was to refine that interpolation with muon data and to extend the borehole-based density model into three dimensions around the shield. Because the inclined detector views the ground above and ahead of the machine, density anomalies can be indicated about 30--50~m in advance of the TBM, giving time for precautionary measures that reduce construction risk.

\subsection{Tunnel segment}
A segment near the Fuzhou Avenue bridge, about 25~m deep, was chosen to illustrate the approach. Several borehole logs were available along this section, and the shield advanced slowly enough that the detector recorded for about one to one and a half days at each of two rings, seven rings apart. This exposure is long compared with normal advance and therefore well suited to a demonstration. Between the two rings the detector moved about 13~m along the tunnel while its height and inclination changed only slightly, so the two measurements view the same ground from different positions. Open-sky reference measurements were acquired with the same detector and track selection.

\subsection{Algorithm design}
The reconstruction starts from a prior density model built from the borehole logs. Layer boundaries are interpolated between boreholes along the tunnel and extended laterally, each layer is assigned a reference density, and the tunnel cavity and lining are modelled explicitly. For a trial density distribution, ray tracing calculates the material traversed by muons arriving from each direction \autocite{siddon1985}, and a muon spectrum \autocite{guan2015} with standard energy-loss ranges \autocite{groom2001} converts this into the expected count. The algorithm then adjusts three-dimensional density corrections, on 2~m voxels, until the expected counts best agree with the measured counts from both rings, accounting for their Poisson statistics.

Two viewing positions cannot determine a three-dimensional density field on their own, so the design relies on prior knowledge. Corrections are penalised more strongly near boreholes, where the ground is known, and smoothing between neighbouring voxels is relaxed across interpreted layer boundaries, where real density contrasts are expected. A common normalisation, estimated from directions away from known bridge piles, accounts for differences between the underground and open-sky datasets. The result is therefore a regularised correction to the borehole model rather than an independent absolute-density image.

\subsection{Results and interpretation}
Figure~\ref{fig:bridge-3d} shows the joint reconstruction from both rings. The main feature is a low-density region above the tunnel and to one side, with a mean correction of $-0.36$~g\,cm$^{-3}$ relative to the borehole model; a smaller positive correction appears near ring 1679. Because sparse views and regularisation can create artefacts, two checks test whether this feature is supported by the data. First, each ring was reconstructed on its own: both show a density deficit in the same region ($-0.21$ and $-0.24$~g\,cm$^{-3}$), so the feature does not depend on a single dataset, although its detailed shape differs. Second, each single-ring reconstruction was used to predict the counts recorded at the other ring. Corrections that only fitted noise would not improve this independent prediction; in both directions they agreed better with the measured counts than the borehole model alone, modestly but consistently.

The result indicates a possible low-density region, not a confirmed void. The main limitations are the short acquisition at each ring, which leaves few counts per direction and makes a stable inversion challenging; sparse borehole control with interpolated layer boundaries; and the non-simultaneous open-sky reference and normalisation.

\section{FIELD DEMONSTRATION II: STATIC MULTI-POSITION PIPELINE INVESTIGATION}
\subsection{Engineering objective}
A disused pipeline, about 17~m deep, appeared on the site map but could not be found at the mapped position during manual investigation. The right-line tunnel under construction was designed to pass only about 0.8~m beneath it, so its actual position was needed to judge the risk of interference. Ground-penetrating radar gave incorrect results, and no other effective method was available, so muography was used to estimate its position.

\subsection{Survey arrangement}
The detector was deployed in the completed left-line tunnel, facing the right line under construction about 15~m away. It was placed in turn at four positions spread over about 11~m along the tunnel, each oriented towards the region where the pipeline was most likely to lie (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}, top), and recorded for about one day at each. A shared open-sky reference was used for all four positions, and the detector poses were surveyed for the reconstruction.

\subsection{Algorithm design}
The workflow separated feature detection from three-dimensional localisation. First, a position-specific U-Net \autocite{ronneberger2015} was trained on simulated directional maps containing pipelines with varied locations and orientations, together with cases containing no pipeline, and reached an area under the ROC curve above 0.95 on simulated test data. Each network converted its field map into relative feature scores, which indicate pipeline-like structure but are not calibrated probabilities. All four field maps contained an extended ridge (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}, bottom). A straight ridge was then fitted to the high-score region in each map, and the measured detector poses transformed the four ridges into backprojection planes in a common tunnel frame. The pipeline axis is the line most consistent with all four planes, with views weighted by ridge quality and a soft preference for a near-horizontal direction reflecting the expected utility geometry.

\subsection{Results and engineering use}
The four views converged on a single, nearly horizontal candidate axis crossing the tunnel alignment at about $47^\circ$ (Figure~\ref{fig:axis}), providing an estimate of the orientation and burial position of the `missing' pipe. Based on this result, the construction team changed the vertical alignment of the designed right-line tunnel, and the shield passed through the region without interference. The pipeline position itself was not verified by boreholes or other direct means; the axis also depends on simulation-trained networks and surveyed detector poses, and no positional uncertainty was estimated.

\section{DISCUSSION}
\subsection{Instrumentation lessons}
The campaign shows that one transportable Micromegas system can support dynamic TBM-mounted and stationary multi-position measurements. Pneumatic isolation kept TBM vibration from the detectors, and the low-consumption gas supply allowed about three months between routine services. Most right-line downtime arose from external power and network interruptions, making site infrastructure part of the deployment design.

\subsection{Integration into construction practice}
Figure~\ref{fig:workflow} summarises how the two demonstrations moved from field measurement to an engineering decision. Both share the same measurement chain but use different interpretation branches, and each output passes through an evidence review before informing a precautionary decision; remaining uncertainty can direct further measurement positions. The same chain can become a routine part of shield construction: a headless detector on the TBM back-up screening the ground ahead, its data combined with borehole logs and TBM operating records, and stationary multi-position surveys from completed tunnels for specific targets such as utilities, shafts or obstructions.

Excavation of one ring takes hours, depending on soil and ground conditions, whereas a usable directional image currently needs about a day of exposure at one position. Quasi-real-time, continuous warning is therefore challenging at this stage, and the system is better suited to targeted screening of known concerns, such as suspected utility crossings or sections with sparse borehole control. Larger or multiple detectors, matched sky references, systematic pose records, automated reconstruction with uncertainty estimates and independently confirmed targets are needed to shorten this interval and make the results quantitative.

\section{CONCLUSIONS}
A three-layer Micromegas system completed sustained field deployment on Shenzhen Metro Line~22. Right-line acquisition availability was 85.2\% overall and 97.2\% after external interruptions were excluded, and platform vibration during TBM operation was not detected inside the isolated enclosure. TBM-mounted measurements extended the borehole model into three dimensions and indicated a possible low-density region above the tunnel. A four-position survey from the completed parallel tunnel estimated the axis of a buried pipeline that conventional methods had failed to locate; the construction team adjusted the tunnel alignment on this basis and passed without interference, although the pipe position was not verified directly. These results show the field feasibility and decision-support value of muography in shield tunnelling; faster imaging, matched reference measurements and uncertainty quantification are the next steps towards routine use.
