\abstract{Unforeseen geological conditions and buried utilities pose major risks to urban shield tunnelling, whereas borehole and surface investigations provide only limited spatial coverage in dense urban areas. Cosmic-ray muography is a passive, non-invasive technique for imaging subsurface density, but its application on construction sites requires instrumentation capable of operating under vibration, elevated temperature, high humidity and restricted access. This paper presents a field-deployable three-layer Micromegas muography system and two applications on Shenzhen Metro Line~22. The system integrates environmental control, pneumatic vibration isolation, a low-consumption gas supply, condition monitoring and dedicated data acquisition for both TBM-mounted and stationary measurements. Mounted on tunnel boring machines (TBMs), it operated at depths of approximately 15--30~m over 1371 rings; on the right line, data-acquisition availability was 85.2\%, or 97.2\% excluding external power and network interruptions, and through repeated TBM advance--pause cycles the reconstructed muon rate remained stable although the raw data rate increased. Muon angular distributions measured at two shield positions were combined with the tunnel geometry and borehole logs to extend the borehole-derived density model into three dimensions, revealing a possible low-density zone above and to one side of the tunnel. In a separate investigation, an abandoned pipeline that ground-penetrating radar (GPR) had failed to locate was surveyed from four stations in the completed parallel tunnel, and its axis was estimated using station-specific U-Net models and multi-view geometric reconstruction. On this basis, the vertical alignment of the tunnel was modified and the shield passed the crossing without interference, although the pipeline position was not verified directly. The deployments demonstrate the field feasibility and decision-support value of muography in tunnel construction, and identify shorter exposure times, matched reference measurements and uncertainty quantification as priorities for further development.}

\chapter{Subsurface imaging in shield tunnelling and pipeline localisation via a robust cosmic-ray muography system: a field study}

\section{INTRODUCTION}
Unforeseen geological conditions and buried utilities are among the principal risks in urban shield tunnelling: weak zones, cavities or obstructions ahead of the tunnel face can interrupt advance and damage adjacent infrastructure. Borehole investigations provide only discrete point data, and surface geophysical surveys are often restricted in dense urban areas. Cosmic-ray muography offers a complementary means of subsurface imaging from within the tunnel. Atmospheric muons penetrate tens to hundreds of metres of soil and rock, and an underground detector measures the angular distribution of the transmitted muon flux; comparison with an open-sky reference yields the opacity, or density integrated along each muon trajectory \autocite{procureur2018}. Combining observations from several viewing directions and detector positions with the tunnel geometry and borehole data enables three-dimensional imaging of density variations around and ahead of the excavation. The technique is passive and non-invasive: it requires no artificial radiation source, surface access or contact with the target, and can operate concurrently with construction.

Previous underground studies have reconstructed overburden density and rock--air interfaces from multiple detector locations \autocite{guardincerri2017}, identified concealed construction shafts and a subsequently confirmed overburden void in a railway tunnel \autocite{thompson2020}, and detected structural changes along a subway station and tunnel \autocite{mao2023}. These studies demonstrate the potential of in-tunnel measurements, but sustained deployment during active shield construction imposes additional instrumentation and operational requirements.

A detector mounted on an operating tunnel boring machine (TBM) is exposed to vibration, heat and humidity in a confined space with limited maintenance access, and its observation geometry changes continuously as the shield advances. Stationary investigations, by contrast, require accurate repositioning of the instrument so that observations of a fixed target from several stations can be combined.

This paper presents a field-deployable three-layer Micromegas muography system that supports both dynamic TBM-mounted measurements and stationary multi-station investigations. The contributions are: (1) an instrument designed for operation under construction conditions; (2) an assessment of its long-term operation on Shenzhen Metro Line~22; and (3) two field demonstrations, namely TBM-mounted ground-density imaging and a four-station investigation of an unlocated buried pipeline. 

\section{TUNNEL-DEPLOYABLE MUOGRAPHY SYSTEM}
\subsection{Detector and measurement principle}
The tracking unit comprises three thermally bonded Micromegas layers, each with a $40\times40$~cm$^2$ sensitive area and separated by approximately 15~cm \autocite{feng2021thermal}. Orthogonal readout strips segmented at 1.6~mm give two-dimensional hit positions on each layer, and each muon trajectory is reconstructed from the hits in all three layers. This fine segmentation provides an angular resolution of about $0.3^\circ$, among the best available for a detector with such a large sensitive area, so that fine angular structure is resolved without sacrificing geometric acceptance. The front-end and data acquisition electronics were developed in-house and customised for muography, forming inter-layer coincidences and aggregating events in a compact readout chain \autocite{wang2025readout,liu2025backend}. Table~\ref{tab:specs} summarises the main specifications of the deployed system.

\subsection{Design for the tunnel environment}
Shield tunnelling exposes the instrument to heat and humidity, machine vibration, a continuously changing observation geometry and limited access for maintenance. The IP56 main enclosure integrates the detectors, readout electronics, power supplies and a sealed air-conditioning unit that controls internal temperature and humidity. For TBM-mounted operation, four pneumatic isolators separate the inclined detector frame from the shield support platform, and vibration sensors on the platform and inside the enclosure record the vibration on each side of the isolators. An inclinometer records the detector inclination so that each measurement can be associated with its observation geometry. A dedicated gas-control subsystem in a separate enclosure supplies the argon--carbon-dioxide mixture with much lower consumption than laboratory flow-gas operation; a single 8~L cylinder at 10~MPa sustained approximately three months of field operation, which set the routine maintenance interval. The system is an unattended (headless) instrument: it runs without an operator on site, and its status, environmental and vibration data are monitored and controlled remotely over Ethernet or RS485, making it suitable for autonomous, long-term sensing in the tunnel. Figure~\ref{fig:installation} shows the enclosure design with dimensions (a), internal arrangement (b) and installation on the TBM (c).

\subsection{Field deployment and operational performance}
The system operated at depths of approximately 15--30~m in the Guihua Station--Songyuanxia Station interval from 6~November~2025 to 27~July~2026, covering 1371 rings (428 on the left line and 943 on the right). It was installed approximately 5~m behind the cutterhead on the left line and 15~m behind it on the right. Detector positions and orientations were associated with the corresponding ring records for subsequent analysis. Monitored enclosure temperatures remained below $40\,^{\circ}$C and relative humidity below 70\%.

On the right line, effective data acquisition totalled 2965.5~h, or 85.2\% of the 3480.0~h monitoring period. The availability was 97.2\% after 401.5~h of external power loss and 28.3~h of network interruption were excluded. The remaining 84.7~h comprised system maintenance.

Figure~\ref{fig:vibration} compares the records on either side of TBM advance--pause transitions between 20 May and 11 July 2026, with advance and pause periods identified from the platform vibration record. When the shield advanced, the platform acceleration rose from near zero to about 8--10~m$g$ and the DAQ data rate increased with it (Figure~\ref{fig:vibration}a,c); over 56 transitions, the data rate during advance was on average 76\% higher than during the adjacent pause (95\% interval 62--93\%; Figure~\ref{fig:vibration}d). The isolators therefore did not fully suppress the disturbance: residual vibration, stronger electromagnetic interference from the TBM, or both induced additional spurious triggers. The rate of reconstructed three-layer muon tracks, by contrast, did not change (Figure~\ref{fig:vibration}b,d): the mean difference between advance and pause was $-1.5$\% (95\% interval $-6.9$ to $+5.8$\%). The three-layer coincidence and track reconstruction thus rejected the spurious triggers, so TBM operation increased the data volume without measurably affecting the muon measurement.

\section{DEMONSTRATION I: DENSITY IMAGING AHEAD OF THE TBM}
\subsection{Engineering objective}
Borehole logs describe the ground only at discrete points, and the strata between them are normally interpolated. The objective of this demonstration was to refine that interpolation with muon data and to extend the borehole-based density model into three dimensions around the shield. Because the inclined detector views the ground above and ahead of the machine, density anomalies can be indicated about 30--50~m in advance of the TBM, giving time for precautionary measures that reduce construction risk.

\subsection{Test section}
A test section near the Fuzhou Avenue bridge, at a depth of approximately 25~m, was selected. Several borehole logs were available along this section, and the low advance rate allowed exposures of approximately 1--1.5 days at each of two rings, seven rings apart. These exposures are long relative to normal advance and therefore well suited to the demonstration. Between the two rings the detector moved approximately 13~m along the tunnel while its elevation and inclination changed only slightly, so the two measurements observe the same ground from different positions. Open-sky reference measurements were acquired with the same detector and track selection.

\subsection{Density inversion method}
The reconstruction starts from a prior density model built from the borehole logs. Layer boundaries are interpolated between boreholes along the tunnel and extended laterally, each layer is assigned a reference density, and the tunnel cavity and lining are modelled explicitly. For a trial density distribution, ray tracing calculates the path length through each material for every muon direction \autocite{siddon1985}, and a muon spectrum \autocite{guan2015} with standard energy-loss ranges \autocite{groom2001} converts this into the expected muon count. The algorithm then adjusts three-dimensional density corrections, on 2~m voxels, until the expected counts best agree with the measured counts from both rings, accounting for their Poisson statistics.

Two viewing positions alone cannot constrain a three-dimensional density field, so the inversion relies on prior information. Corrections are penalised more strongly near boreholes, where the ground is known, and smoothing between neighbouring voxels is relaxed across interpreted layer boundaries, where real density contrasts are expected. A common normalisation, estimated from directions away from known bridge piles, accounts for differences between the underground and open-sky datasets. The result is therefore a regularised correction to the borehole model rather than an independent absolute-density image.

\subsection{Results and interpretation}
Figure~\ref{fig:bridge-3d} shows the joint reconstruction from both rings. The principal feature is a low-density zone above the tunnel and to one side, with a mean correction of $-0.36$~g\,cm$^{-3}$ relative to the borehole model; a smaller positive correction appears near ring 1679. Because sparse viewpoints and regularisation can create artefacts, two checks test whether this feature is supported by the data. First, each ring was reconstructed on its own: both show a density deficit in the same region ($-0.21$ and $-0.24$~g\,cm$^{-3}$), so the feature does not depend on a single dataset, although its detailed shape differs. Second, each single-ring reconstruction was used to predict the counts recorded at the other ring. Corrections fitted only to statistical fluctuations would not improve this independent prediction; in both directions they agreed better with the measured counts than the borehole model alone, modestly but consistently.

The result indicates a possible low-density zone rather than a confirmed void. The main limitations are the short exposure at each ring, which limits the counts per angular bin and challenges the stability of the inversion; sparse borehole control with interpolated layer boundaries; and the non-simultaneous open-sky reference and normalisation.

\section{DEMONSTRATION II: DISUSED PIPELINE LOCALISATION}
\subsection{Engineering objective}
An abandoned pipeline, buried approximately 17~m deep, was recorded on the site utility map but could not be found at the mapped position during site investigation. The right-line tunnel under construction was designed to pass only about 0.8~m beneath it, so its actual position was needed to judge the risk of interference. Ground-penetrating radar surveys failed to locate it correctly, and no other effective method was available; muography was therefore used to estimate its position.

\subsection{Survey arrangement}
The detector was deployed in the completed left-line tunnel, facing the right line under construction about 15~m away. It was placed in turn at four stations spanning approximately 11~m along the tunnel, each oriented towards the region where the pipeline was most likely to lie (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}, top), with an exposure of approximately one day at each. A shared open-sky reference was used for all four stations, and the detector poses were surveyed for the reconstruction.

\subsection{Pipeline reconstruction method}
The workflow separated feature detection from three-dimensional localisation. First, a station-specific U-Net \autocite{ronneberger2015} was trained on simulated muon angular distributions containing pipelines with varied locations and orientations, together with cases containing no pipeline, and reached an area under the ROC curve above 0.95 on simulated test data. Each network converted the measured angular distribution into relative feature scores, which indicate pipeline-like structure but are not calibrated probabilities. All four measured score maps contained an extended ridge (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}, bottom). A straight ridge was then fitted to the high-score region in each map, and the measured detector poses transformed the four ridges into backprojection planes in a common tunnel frame. The pipeline axis is the line most consistent with all four planes, with observations weighted by ridge quality and a soft preference for a near-horizontal direction reflecting the expected utility geometry.

\subsection{Results and engineering use}
The four observations converged on a single, nearly horizontal candidate axis crossing above the tunnel alignment (Figure~\ref{fig:axis}), providing an estimate of the orientation and burial depth of the unlocated pipeline. On this basis, the vertical alignment of the right-line tunnel was modified, and the shield passed the crossing without interference. The pipeline position itself was not verified by boreholes or other direct means; the axis also depends on simulation-trained networks and surveyed detector poses, and no positional uncertainty was estimated.

\section{DISCUSSION}
\subsection{Lessons for field instrumentation}
The campaign shows that one transportable Micromegas system can support dynamic TBM-mounted and stationary multi-station measurements. TBM advance raised the raw data rate despite pneumatic isolation, but three-layer track reconstruction kept the muon rate unaffected; separating the vibration and electromagnetic contributions is a design task for future systems. In addition, the low-consumption gas supply allowed about three months between routine services. Most right-line downtime arose from external power and network interruptions, making site infrastructure part of the deployment design.

\subsection{Integration into tunnelling practice}
Figure~\ref{fig:workflow} summarises how the two demonstrations moved from field measurement to an engineering decision. Both share the same measurement chain but use different interpretation branches, and each output passes through an evidence review before informing a precautionary decision; remaining uncertainty can direct further measurement stations. The same chain can become a routine part of shield construction: an unattended detector on the TBM back-up gantry screening the ground ahead of the face, its data combined with borehole logs and TBM operating records, and stationary multi-station surveys from completed tunnels for specific targets such as utilities, shafts or obstructions.

Excavation of one ring takes hours, depending on soil and ground conditions, whereas a statistically adequate angular distribution currently requires about one day of exposure at each station. Quasi-real-time, continuous warning is therefore challenging at this stage, and the system is better suited to targeted screening of known concerns, such as suspected utility crossings or sections with sparse borehole control. Larger or multiple detectors, matched sky references, systematic pose records, automated reconstruction with uncertainty estimates and independently confirmed targets are needed to shorten this interval and make the results quantitative.

\section{CONCLUSIONS}
A three-layer Micromegas system completed sustained field deployment on Shenzhen Metro Line~22. Right-line acquisition availability was 85.2\% overall and 97.2\% after external interruptions were excluded, and the reconstructed muon rate was unaffected by TBM advance although the raw data rate increased. TBM-mounted measurements extended the borehole-derived density model into three dimensions and revealed a possible low-density zone above the tunnel. A four-station survey from the completed parallel tunnel estimated the axis of a buried pipeline that conventional methods had failed to locate; the tunnel alignment was modified on this basis and the shield passed the crossing without interference, although the pipeline position was not verified directly. These results demonstrate the field feasibility and decision-support value of muography in shield tunnelling; shorter exposure times, matched reference measurements and uncertainty quantification are the next steps towards routine application.
